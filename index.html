Read this first — three things change your strategy

Everything below is built on your actual constraints. These three findings matter more than any single bid list.

1. Your B license goes further as a prime than as a sub

B&P Code §7057(b) sets two different tests, and this is the thing to get right. Most people assume the rule is the same either way. It isn't — and the asymmetry runs opposite to intuition.

Take a single-trade job — landscaping only, roofing only, fencing only, painting only, concrete only:

As PRIME You can bid it

Allowed if any one of these is true:

  • the prime contract requires two or more unrelated trades other than framing/carpentry, or
  • you hold the matching C classification, or
  • you subcontract the work to an appropriately licensed contractor.

That third route is the one that matters. It is written into the statute.

As SUBCONTRACTOR Not without the C

Allowed only if:

  • the subcontract itself requires two or more unrelated trades other than framing/carpentry, or
  • you hold the matching C classification.

Subbing it back out is not available on this side. This is the only place a qualifier actually changes what you can take.

Framing or carpentry alone is fine either way — prime or sub, any size.

So yes — you can bid single-trade landscaping, fencing, painting and roofing jobs, as the prime, by carrying a properly licensed specialty subcontractor for that scope. That is written into the statute, not a workaround. It is how a great many general contractors operate.

The real restriction is narrower than it first looks: it bites when you want to be somebody else's sub on a single-trade package. A landscaping-only subcontract under a school modernization prime needs a C-27 in your own name. That is the gap a qualifier would close.

The gate that actually decides most bids is not the statute — it is the solicitation. Agencies name the acceptable classifications in the bid documents. If it says "C-27 required," you are out no matter what §7057 permits. If it says "A, B or C-27," or just a valid California contractor's licence, you are in. Read the license requirement on every landscaping and single-trade job before ruling it in or out. Note also that routine landscape maintenance — mowing, groundskeeping, trimming — is often not licensed contracting work at all and gets let as a service contract.

One business caveat, separate from the law: priming a single-trade job you sub out 100% means carrying the bond, the DIR registration and the performance risk for a markup on someone else's price. Worth doing when the margin or the relationship justifies it; worth pricing honestly when it doesn't.

On public works, whichever route you take, Public Contract Code §4104 requires primes to list each sub by name, license number and classification — and agencies check the classification against the scope. Your C-27 sub has to be real and named.

If and when a classification is worth carrying

Your plan is to pick up any needed C through an RMO rather than qualifying it in-house, so the decision is a staffing one, driven by pipeline volume — not a task on this list. Nothing in this playbook assumes you hold anything beyond the B.

What a classification would actually buy you, given the table above, is single-trade subcontract work — being the landscaping sub on someone else's modernization job, the fencing sub on a park build. Prime work in those trades is already open to you today by carrying a licensed sub. So the question to track is how much single-trade sub volume you are turning away, not how many landscaping jobs exist.

Two things worth knowing when you get there, so the arrangement holds up: CSLB expects the qualifier to exercise actual direct supervision and control over the classification they qualify (B&P §7068.1), and nominal qualifier arrangements are treated as a serious violation for both the firm and the individual. There are also limits on how many licenses one person may qualify at once, and a Bond of Qualifying Individual may be required depending on the qualifier's ownership stake. Worth confirming the current specifics with CSLB or your license attorney before you structure it.

The practical takeaway for now: log every bid you screen out for want of a classification. That running count is what tells you whether the RMO is worth it, and for which trade.

2. Your best federal advantage is real: WOSB covers construction

A common myth is that the SBA's Women-Owned Small Business set-aside program excludes construction. It does not. The eligible-industry list was expanded to 759 industries and includes 236220 (commercial/institutional building), the entire 238xxx specialty trade series (concrete 238110, roofing 238160, HVAC/plumbing 238220, painting 238320, site prep/demolition 238910, other specialty/fencing 238990), and 561730 (landscaping).

WOSB certification is free and lets you compete in set-aside competitions that are closed to firms without it. Because you're not veteran-owned, this is your primary federal socioeconomic lever — and it's a strong one. Apply at certifications.sba.gov after your SAM.gov registration is active.

3. The DBE program changed — women are no longer automatically presumed disadvantaged

DBE certification is still the single highest-value certification for transportation/transit/airport work (one Caltrans-issued cert is honored by LA Metro, SANDAG, San Diego MTS, NCTD, RCTC, RTA, LAWA and San Diego International). But a USDOT Interim Final Rule effective October 3, 2025 eliminated the automatic race- and sex-based presumption of social and economic disadvantage. Every applicant — including women — must now submit an individualized Personal Narrative documenting specific instances of hardship and denied opportunity, plus an Economic Impact Statement and Personal Net Worth statement.

California's CUCP completed its mandatory reevaluation of existing DBEs and reopened for new applications in 2026. It is still very much worth pursuing — just budget real time for the narrative, and get an APEX Accelerator counselor to help you write it (free).

Where the winnable work actually is

Ranked by realistic win probability for a small firm without a long public-works past-performance record.

  1. CUPCCAA informal bid lists
    Under the Uniform Public Construction Cost Accounting Act (PCC §22032, current as of 1/1/2025): agencies can do jobs up to $75,000 by force account/negotiated purchase, and up to $220,000 by informal bid — inviting only contractors on their qualified list. Getting on those lists is usually a one-page annual application. This is the single biggest shortcut in California public works and almost nobody small bothers to do it systematically. Do it for every agency in your radius.
  2. JOC / on-call / task-order pools
    Get prequalified once, then receive repeat work orders for years without rebidding. Riverside County Facilities Management runs JOC lanes specifically for painting (C-33), roofing (C-39) and HVAC (C-20). LA County ISD, LA County DPW, City of San Diego "As-Needed" contracts, LAUSD, UCLA and UC systemwide all run JOC programs.
  3. Subcontracting to primes with outreach obligations
    On federally-funded and City of LA work, primes are legally required to document good-faith outreach to certified small/women/disadvantaged subs. Certified firms get called for quotes. Uncertified firms don't.
  4. Small formal prime bids ($220K–$1M)
    Realistic once you have bonding capacity and 2–3 completed public jobs. Below $1M you also usually avoid school-district prequalification.
  5. Large formal prime bids
    Not yet. Prequalification, skilled-and-trained-workforce rules and bonding will screen you out. Come back in 2 years.

Two structural barriers to plan around

Skilled & Trained Workforce requirements — on many large California public projects, a set percentage of each apprenticeable trade's hours must be performed by graduates of a state-approved apprenticeship program. For a small non-union shop this is often disqualifying. It generally does not apply to smaller jobs, which is another reason to stay under the formal-bid threshold at first.

Project Labor Agreements — LAUSD, LA County, LA Metro, LACCD and the City of LA operate under PLAs. You can still work on PLA jobs as a non-union contractor, but you must sign onto the PLA terms and typically hire through union halls for covered trades. Read the PLA before bidding; it changes your cost model.

What the screening applies

Prime and subcontract are different tests under §7057(b). As prime, a single-trade job is biddable by subcontracting that scope to an appropriately licensed contractor — so landscaping, fencing, painting and roofing primes are in. As somebody else's sub on a single-trade package, the matching C is required and that route is closed. What decides most bids in practice is the licence line in the solicitation itself.

What genuinely eliminates the largest share is Class A General Engineering — paving, roadway reconstruction, resurfacing, slurry seal, right-of-way curb and gutter, channels and reservoirs. Subcontracting does not cure that one.

Where to find better rows

Bid aggregators skew heavily toward heavy civil work. The higher-yield sources for a Class B are school district and city facilities departments — modernizations, re-roofs, restroom renovations, portable classrooms. Get onto the CUPCCAA informal bidders lists and JOC pools in the Where to Bid tab and this work starts arriving without you hunting for it.

One move that widens the board immediately: line up a licensed sub in each core trade — a C-27 landscaper, a C-13 fence contractor, a C-39 roofer, a C-21 demo contractor — who will price for you on 48 hours' notice. That relationship is what turns a single-trade prime solicitation into a bid you can actually submit.

Getting subcontract work

Why certified subs get called

On any federally-funded project with a DBE goal, and on every City of Los Angeles contract under the Business Inclusion Program, the prime contractor must document good-faith outreach to certified small/women/disadvantaged subcontractors — and can lose the award if they can't. On large federal contracts, FAR 52.219-9 requires primes to maintain a subcontracting plan with specific percentage goals for WOSB participation. Primes are not being charitable when they call you; they need you on their compliance paperwork. Being certified and easy to find is the whole game.

The script

Call the estimating department, not the front desk. Ask for "the estimator who handles [trade] subcontractor bids." Then:

"Hi, I'm [name] with [company]. We're a woman-owned small business, CSLB Class B, [certifications], based in [city]. We self-perform [trades] on public works in [counties]. I'd like to be added to your bid invitation list and get your subcontractor prequalification packet. Can I email you our capability statement?"

Then follow up every time you see them listed as a bidder on a job in your area. Bidder lists are public on every agency portal.

The three findings that change the strategy

1. The B license alone does NOT allow subcontracting "any trade needed"

Per B&P Code §7057 and CSLB's published Class B scope, a General Building contractor may take a prime contract or a subcontract for framing/carpentry freely. For any other single trade — painting, roofing, fencing, concrete, landscaping, HVAC, plumbing, demolition — the contract must involve at least two unrelated trades other than framing or carpentry, OR the contractor must hold the matching C license, OR must subcontract that work to a properly licensed specialty contractor. Framing and carpentry cannot be counted toward the two-trade test.

On public works this is enforced: PCC §4104 requires primes to list each subcontractor with license number and classification, and agencies verify the classification matches the scope of work.

In practice: the clean lanes on the B alone are framing/carpentry subcontracts at any size, and multi-trade prime jobs where some scopes are self-performed and the rest subbed to licensed specialists. A solicitation that is purely one trade (landscaping C-27, demolition C-21, concrete C-8, painting C-33, roofing C-39, fencing C-13) is a no-bid unless the matching qualifier is carried. MPX's approach is to add a classification through an RMO if and when pipeline volume justifies it — so the standing action is to log every bid screened out for want of a classification, and let that count drive the decision rather than a guess.

When structuring an RMO arrangement: CSLB expects the qualifier to exercise actual direct supervision and control over the classification (B&P §7068.1), there are limits on how many licenses one person may qualify at once, and a Bond of Qualifying Individual may be required depending on the qualifier's ownership stake. Confirm current specifics with CSLB or a license attorney.

2. Federal WOSB set-asides DO cover construction — this is the main advantage

The SBA WOSB/EDWOSB eligible-industry list (expanded to 759 industries) includes 236220, the entire 238xxx specialty trade series (concrete 238110, roofing 238160, HVAC/plumbing 238220, painting 238320, site prep/demolition 238910, other specialty/fencing 238990) and 561730 landscaping. Free certification at certifications.sba.gov. This is the primary federal socioeconomic lever available given non-veteran status.

3. DBE no longer presumes women are disadvantaged

USDOT Interim Final Rule effective October 3, 2025 eliminated the automatic race- and sex-based presumption in the DBE/ACDBE programs. All applicants — including women — must now submit an individualized Personal Narrative plus Economic Impact Statement plus Personal Net Worth statement. California's CUCP completed mandatory reevaluation of existing DBEs and reopened for new applications in 2026.

DBE remains the single highest-value certification (one Caltrans-issued cert honored by LA Metro, SANDAG, MTS, NCTD, RCTC, RTA, LAWA, San Diego International) — but it now requires real narrative work. Get APEX Accelerator help writing it, free. Caps: personal net worth < $2,047,000; 3-yr average receipts < ~$31.8M.

4. WOSB / EDWOSB Federal Set-Aside Program — Construction NAICS Eligibility

Confirmed accurate finding: YES, construction NAICS codes ARE eligible for the WOSB/EDWOSB program, and this is a major point in the contractor's favor. This was cross-verified across two independent sources (a WBENC-published qualifying NAICS list and a Federal Register notice on the WOSB eligible-industries update):

  • The SBA is statutorily required to re-study and update the WOSB eligible-industries list every 5 years. A recent update expanded the eligible list from 444 to 759 industries (~70% increase).
  • Of the 759 total eligible industries: 646 are open to WOSB Program competition generally (any SBA-certified WOSB), and 113 are reserved for EDWOSB-only competition.
  • Confirmed construction NAICS codes on the eligible list (WOSB-eligible tier, "Table 3" per the source document) include:
  • 236115, 236116, 236117 (new housing construction, various)
  • 236118 (Residential Remodelers)
  • 236210 (Industrial Building Construction)
  • 236220 (Commercial and Institutional Building Construction) — directly relevant to client
  • 237110, 237120, 237130 (utility line construction)
  • 237310 (Highway, Street, and Bridge Construction)
  • 237990 (Other Heavy and Civil Engineering Construction)
  • All specialty trade contractor codes in the 238-xxx series (238110 through 238990) are WOSB-eligible, per the same sources — this directly covers concrete (238110), roofing (238160), painting (238320), site prep/demolition (238910), and the catch-all fencing/other specialty trade code (238990).
  • 561730 (Landscaping Services) is confirmed WOSB/EDWOSB-eligible, specifically flagged in the source as eligible for EDWOSB set-aside and sole-source awards.

2. DGS Small Business (SB) & SB-PW Certification

Why this matters for your firm

SB/SB-PW certification is the single highest-leverage credential available to you as a non-veteran, woman-owned B-license firm — it is open regardless of veteran status (unlike DVBE), and it unlocks bid preferences, set-asides, and sole-source authority.

Eligibility (both programs share these baseline criteria)

  • Independently owned and operated; not dominant in its field
  • Principal office located in California
  • All owners, officers, and managing members/partners must be domiciled in California

Program Max Employees (incl. affiliates) Max Avg. Gross Annual Receipts (3-yr avg) Where it counts
SB 100 ~$19 million All state contract types (goods, services, IT, AND public works)
SB-PW (Small Business for Public Works) 200 ~$46 million Public works contracts only

A firm below $6M average receipts (or a manufacturer with ≤25 employees) is additionally classified as a Microbusiness (MB), which carries its own set-aside advantages.

Why SB-PW matters even though it doesn't stack with SB

If your gross receipts or employee count are between the SB cap and the SB-PW cap, SB-PW lets you still get public-works-specific benefits (bid preference, set-aside eligibility, and counting toward a prime's small-business subcontracting goal) even if you're "too big" for general SB. Most very small B-license shops will simply qualify as SB (which is valid for public works too), making SB-PW most relevant to growing firms.

Benefits

  1. 5% bid preference in the evaluation of competitive bids — for low-price awards, 5% of the lowest non-SB bid is notionally subtracted from your bid for ranking purposes (does not change contract price). Capped at $50,000 per bid, or combined with other preferences, capped at 15% or $100,000, whichever is less.
  2. SB/DVBE Option (PCC §2-2 / State Contracting Manual Ch. 12-07) — a streamlined, non-fully-competitive acquisition method state agencies may use to award directly to a certified SB or DVBE, provided at least two SB/DVBE quotes are obtained (or one, under some conditions) — dollar threshold for public works under this method is $461,000 per a 2024 DGS Broadcast Bulletin (BB-P0224). [NOTE: DGS issued a further threshold-consolidation bulletin, BB-P-10-25, effective through end of 2025, that may have changed this figure again — confirm the current number with DGS OSDS before relying on it, as I could not access the specific updated public-works figure.]
  3. Sole-source authority to certified SBs — California law (referenced in DGS OSDS materials) allows agencies to award directly to a certified small business without full competitive bidding up to a set dollar cap. Commonly cited as up to $250,000, but I was unable to independently verify the exact current statutory citation/number through primary source text in this session (the specific PCC section I attempted, §12102/12102.1, turned out to govern IT acquisitions, not the general SB sole-source rule) — flag this as unverified; confirm directly with DGS OSDS (OSDShelp@dgs.ca.gov) before quoting this number to anyone.
  4. 25% small business participation goal — most state agencies (Caltrans included) have an overall goal that at least 25% of contract dollars go to SB/SB-PW firms, and 3% to DVBE. This creates real pull-through demand for prime contractors to find and use SB subs — you should market yourself directly to primes as "DGS-certified SB, counts toward your 25% goal."
  5. Priority/first notification on solicitations under the SB set-aside process, and prompt payment provisions.

  1. Register on Cal eProcure (Section 1).
  2. Submit the SB/SB-PW application through the Cal eProcure certification module — no fee.
  3. Upload: 3 years of tax returns/financials (to prove gross receipts), CSLB license, proof of CA domicile/principal office, and an SB/DVBE Declaration.
  4. Processing typically takes 60–90 days; certification is valid 2 years, with a required biennial re-application.
  5. Once certified, your firm appears in the State of California certified-firm search tool at caleprocure.ca.gov (public search), so primes actively looking for SB subs can find you — keep your profile's trade/commodity codes accurate and complete.

On DVBE — accuracy note per your brief

Your firm is not veteran-owned, so it cannot be DVBE-certified and cannot itself claim the DVBE bid incentive or count toward a DVBE participation goal. Caltrans DBE Program & CUCP Certification

The federal DBE (Disadvantaged Business Enterprise) program is distinct from the state's SB/DVBE programs — it applies to federal-aid highway/transit projects (many Caltrans jobs use FHWA money) and is certified through the California Unified Certification Program (CUCP), a joint certification recognized by Caltrans and virtually all CA transit agencies (Metro, SANDAG/MTS, Metrolink, etc.) statewide.

Eligibility for DBE (a woman-owned firm is presumptively eligible for the "socially and economically disadvantaged" test — women are a statutorily recognized group under 49 CFR Part 26): - Firm is at least 51% owned and controlled (management + daily operations) by a socially/economically disadvantaged individual — women qualify under the federal rebuttable presumption. - Owner's personal net worth ≤ $2,047,000 (excludes retirement accounts, primary residence equity, and equity in the business itself). - Firm's average annual gross receipts (3-yr avg) do not exceed $31,840,000 (NAICS-specific caps can vary; construction is generally under this cap). - Owner must be a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident.

Application: Apply via the DBE certification system at californiaucp.dbesystem.com (the CUCP's unified online system — one application covers certification recognized by Caltrans and other CA agencies). 5. Job Order Contracting (JOC) — Why It's a Great Fit for You

What JOC is: JOC is an indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity (IDIQ) contracting vehicle. Instead of the agency bidding out each small project individually, it competitively selects a small "bench" of pre-qualified contractors (sometimes just one, sometimes several) under a master contract with pre-negotiated unit prices (usually from a published cost book / "Construction Task Catalog," e.g., RSMeans-based). When the agency has a project — a re-roof, a fence replacement, a parking lot demo, an HVAC swap-out, an ADA ramp — it issues a Job Order (task order) directly to a bench contractor, who prices it using the pre-agreed unit prices and a coefficient/markup, with no separate public bid for that individual job (each Job Order is capped by a "minor capital" dollar threshold, commonly in the low millions).

Where it's used in your target area: CSU (systemwide JOC program, campus-specific benches — e.g., SDSU, CSUSM, Cal Poly Pomona), likely UC campuses, and many community college districts (LACCD, RCCD, SDCCD) all run JOC or JOC-style annual/on-call contractor pools for minor capital and facilities work.

Why it fits a B-license small firm well: 1. You only have to win the bid/RFQ once (to get on the bench, usually a 1–3 year term, sometimes with renewal options), then you get repeat work without re-bidding every project. 2. Individual job orders are typically well under your $5M target — often in the $10K–$2M range — squarely inside general B-license authority. 3. Multi-trade nature matches your B license perfectly. A JOC contractor is expected to self-perform or manage a range of trades (concrete, fencing, painting, roofing, minor demo, etc.) — exactly the "two or more unrelated trades" profile a B license is built for (see Section 6). 4. Lower bonding/overhead burden than chasing large discrete public bids — you bond per job order (often smaller amounts) rather than one giant lump-sum contract. 5. Small business set-asides are common within JOC programs — some agencies run a dedicated SB/DVBE JOC bench, or give SB bid preference in the JOC-bench selection process itself.

How to pursue: Watch each target campus/district's procurement portal (PlanetBids, CalUsource, etc.) for periodic "JOC Bench," "Minor Capital / Blanket," or "On-Call Contractor" solicitations (e.g., the CSUSM "26/27 Blanket Minor Cap Advertisement" found above is exactly this kind of solicitation) — these come up on a cycle (often annually or every 2–3 years), so once you find a program you're interested in, set a calendar reminder to watch for its next re-solicitation.

6. Public Works Compliance Requirements You MUST Satisfy

DIR Public Works Contractor Registration (Labor Code §1725.5)

Mandatory for any contractor or subcontractor bidding or working on a California public works project, regardless of contract size. - Register/renew at DIR's Public Works Contractor Registration portal: https://www.dir.ca.gov/public-works/contractor-registration.html - Fees: $400 (1 fiscal year, July 1–June 30) / $800 (2 years) / $1,200 (3 years). - Requirements to register: current CSLB license in good standing, active workers' compensation coverage, no delinquent wage/penalty assessments, not on federal or state debarment lists, and (if you subcontract) you may only use other DIR-registered subcontractors. - Credit card payment processes registration within ~24 hours; other payment methods can take up to 8 weeks — pay by card if you need to bid soon. - You must re-register every fiscal year you intend to work public works.

Prevailing Wage & Certified Payroll (eCPR)

  • All public works labor must be paid at the DIR-determined prevailing wage rate for the trade/county/craft.
  • Contractors must submit certified payroll records electronically via DIR's eCPR (electronic Certified Payroll Reporting) system for every public works project — this is now the standard statewide requirement tied to your DIR registration.
  • Failure to pay prevailing wage or file certified payroll properly can result in penalties, forfeitures, and registration revocation.

Apprenticeship Requirements (DAS-140 / DAS-142)

  • On public works contracts, contractors in an "apprenticeable craft or trade" must comply with California apprenticeship law.
  • DAS-140 ("Contract Award Information") must be filed with the applicable apprenticeship committee within 10 days of contract award, notifying them you've been awarded a public works job so apprentices can be dispatched.
  • DAS-142 ("Request for Dispatch of an Apprentice") is filed to formally request apprentice(s) be dispatched to the job.
  • Contractors must employ apprentices at the statutory ratio and, if not using apprentices, may owe training fund contributions in lieu.
  • Details: https://www.dir.ca.gov/public-works/apprentices.html and https://dir.ca.gov/das/publicworks.html

CSLB B-License Classification Limits (Business & Professions Code §7057) — Read Carefully

This is the single most important legal constraint on your ability to act as prime: - A General Building (B) contractor's principal business must involve structures requiring at least two unrelated building trades or crafts. - Critical rule: framing and carpentry CANNOT be counted as one of the two unrelated trades needed to qualify a project for a B-license prime. In other words, you cannot take a prime contract that is essentially a single specialty trade (e.g., "just roofing," "just fencing," "just painting," "just concrete flatwork," "just HVAC") merely by adding framing/carpentry as your "second trade" — the law explicitly excludes that from counting. - Practical effect for your trade list: If a project genuinely calls for two or more of landscaping, concrete, painting, fencing, roofing, demolition, HVAC, and plumbing (e.g., a park renovation needing new fencing AND concrete walkways AND landscaping), your B license can legally take it as prime and self-perform or sub out the individual trades. - If a solicitation is truly single-trade (e.g., a pure re-roofing job, a pure HVAC replacement, a pure fence-only contract), a B license cannot be the prime unless you also hold the relevant C-classification (e.g., C-39 Roofing, C-20 HVAC, C-13 Fencing, C-36 Plumbing, C-27 Landscaping, C-8 Concrete) or you bid/perform as a subcontractor to a prime who holds the right classification — this is why chasing single-trade CDCR/CMD/campus task orders often makes more sense for you as a sub, while multi-trade renovation/park/demolition-and-rebuild projects are your best prime targets. - Note: the classifications that would most often unlock single-trade primes in this market are C-27 Landscaping, C-8 Concrete and C-13 Fencing. Whether to carry any of them is a pipeline-volume decision, and MPX's route would be an RMO rather than qualifying in-house.

Bonding

  • Bid bond: typically 10% of the bid amount, required at time of bid submission on most public works IFBs.
  • Performance bond: typically 100% of the contract price, guaranteeing completion.
  • Payment bond: typically 100% of the contract price, guaranteeing payment to subs/suppliers/laborers.
  • Bonding capacity (your surety's aggregate limit) will functionally cap how large a prime contract you can pursue — build a banking/surety relationship early and grow your bonding line as you complete smaller public jobs successfully.

Insurance

  • Standard public works requirement: Commercial General Liability, typically $1,000,000 per occurrence / $2,000,000 aggregate (some agencies require $2M/$4M for larger or higher-risk scopes).
  • Workers' Compensation per California statutory limits (mandatory to even register with DIR).
  • Automobile liability (commonly $1M combined single limit).
  • The awarding agency (state, county, university) is almost always required to be named as additional insured on your CGL policy for the duration of the project.

2. SBA 8(a) Business Development Program

[VERIFIED LIVE] Eligibility: firm ≥2 years old, ≥51% owned/controlled by a U.S. citizen who is socially and economically disadvantaged; owner's personal net worth ≤$850K, adjusted gross income ≤$400K (2-yr average), total assets ≤$6.5M; firm must meet SBA small-business size standard.

Important nuance the contractor should understand: 8(a)'s racial/ethnic presumption was never sex-based. B. CSLB CLASS B LICENSE SCOPE

[VERIFIED LIVE] — sourced from CSLB's official "General Building" classification page and CSLB's official FastFacts PDF, both tied back to Business & Professions Code §7057.

Statutory definition (B&P §7057): A general building contractor's business is the construction of structures requiring "the use of at least two unrelated building trades or crafts." Merely furnishing materials does not count as contracting.

As a PRIME contractor, a B license holder may: - Take a prime contract for framing or carpentry work alone and self-perform it (special carve-out — no 2-trade requirement needed). - Take a prime contract involving two or more separate and unrelated trades and self-perform them — but framing/carpentry cannot be counted as one of the two trades when combining with others. In other words: framing+carpentry alone = fine as prime; framing+carpentry+one other trade does NOT satisfy the "two unrelated trades" test — she'd need a second trade beyond framing/carpentry to hit two. - Solar installation self-performance is expressly recognized by CSLB (per 16 CCR §832.62(b)) as satisfying the multi-trade requirement. - She may not take a prime contract limited to a single specialty trade outside framing/carpentry (e.g., a job that is only painting, or only fencing, or only landscaping) unless she holds the specific C classification for that trade, or she subcontracts that scope to a properly licensed specialty contractor. Correction (8/15/2026): an earlier draft of this report added a condition that her own scope must still involve qualifying multi-trade work. That condition is not in the statute. B&P §7057(b) permits a general building contractor to take a single-trade prime contract outright so long as the work is subcontracted to an appropriately licensed contractor — so single-trade landscaping, fencing, painting and roofing primes are open to her today. - She cannot take on fire protection (C-16) or well drilling (C-57) work regardless, without those specific licenses.

As a SUBCONTRACTOR — this is the critical finding for her stated plan. CSLB's own classification page states the rule applies symmetrically to subcontracts: "Cannot take subcontracts in other trades unless meeting the two-unrelated-trades requirement or holding proper classifications." This applies to subcontracts specifically, and the prime-side rule is different — see the correction below. §7057(b) sets two separate tests: a prime contract may be single-trade if the work is subcontracted to an appropriately licensed contractor, while a subcontract may not — there the general building contractor must either hold the matching classification or the subcontract itself must span two unrelated trades. So the real limit is on taking single-trade work as somebody else's sub, not on priming it. A B-license holder cannot lawfully take a subcontract that is only painting, only fencing, only roofing, only concrete, only landscaping, only HVAC, or only plumbing — those each require holding the matching C classification (or the sub-scope must itself involve two or more unrelated trades, e.g., a scope that combines concrete flatwork AND fencing installation as one combined multi-trade subcontract package, which is a narrower, less common scenario). Framing and general demolition-as-site-prep are the practical exceptions she can already legitimately pursue as prime or sub on her B license alone, since framing/carpentry is explicitly carved out, and general demolition scopes often qualify as a "unrelated trade" component when paired with site work/grading (though pure single-trade demolition subcontracts should be checked case by case — flag for confirmation with CSLB or counsel on borderline demo scopes).

A PLA does not legally bar a non-union small sub from bidding or working the job — but it requires the contractor to operate under the project's master labor agreement terms for the duration: using the union hiring hall/referral process for covered crafts, paying into the project's union trust/benefit funds for that work, and following grievance/dispute procedures set by the agreement. In practice this raises administrative complexity and cost and can be a real deterrent for a small non-union firm unfamiliar with hiring-hall dispatch — worth confirming PLA status on any specific bid before pricing it, since it changes both cost and staffing logistics materially. CUPCCAA (Uniform Public Construction Cost Accounting Act) — the biggest practical shortcut for a small firm

[VERIFIED LIVE — current statutory thresholds, Public Contract Code §22032, effective January 1, 2025 per AB 2192]:

Project value Procurement method
$75,000 or less May be performed by agency's own forces ("force account"), by negotiated contract, or by purchase order — no formal bidding needed.
$75,000.01 – $220,000 May be let by informal bidding procedures
Above $220,000 Must use formal bidding procedures (published notice, sealed bids, bonding, etc.)

Why the informal tier ($75K–$220K) is a huge shortcut: Agencies that have adopted CUPCCAA (nearly all California cities, counties, and special districts have) must maintain a list of qualified/pre-approved contractors, organized by trade category, republished/re-noticed at least annually via a notice to construction trade journals. Any licensed contractor can request to be added to that list at any time — it's not a competitive process to get onto the list, just an application/request. Once on it, the agency is required to solicit bids from that list (and from the trade papers) for every informal-tier project in her trade categories going forward — meaning she gets automatically invited to bid on a running stream of smaller jobs without having to hunt for each opportunity individually, and without facing the full formal-bid competitive pool (bonding is sometimes still required at the agency's discretion, but the overall barrier to entry — publication requirements, bid-bond thresholds, general competition — is much lower than formal bidding).

Action item: She should identify every city, county, school district, water district, and special district in San Diego, Riverside, and LA counties whose territory she wants to work in, and proactively request to be added to each one's CUPCCAA informal-bid contractors list for her trade categories (general building, landscaping, concrete, demolition, etc., matching whatever classifications she holds). This is free, low-effort per agency, and — because most small firms never bother — puts her ahead of a huge share of the competition for exactly the size of jobs ($75K–$220K) that are realistic first wins for a firm her size.
